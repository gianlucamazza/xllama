The following notes describe how a regional railway timetable is put together. Read them and then answer the three questions at the end. A timetable for a single-track regional line is less a list of departure times than a solution to a packing problem. Trains running in opposite directions can only pass each other where a second track exists, usually at a station with a passing loop, and the distance between loops fixes the minimum interval at which services can alternate. If two loops are twenty minutes of running time apart, a train that leaves one loop late forces the opposing train to wait at the other, and that delay then travels down the line in both directions. Planners therefore begin with the crossings, not with the departures: they decide at which loops trains will meet in each hour, and only then read off the times at the terminals. The second constraint is the rolling stock. A line with an hourly service and a round trip of two hours and ten minutes needs three trainsets, not two, because the ten minutes of overrun cannot be recovered by turning the train around faster than the driver can walk its length, change ends and complete the brake test. Shortening the round trip to under two hours by removing one lightly used stop would release an entire trainset, which is often worth more to the operator than the handful of passengers at that stop are worth in fares. This is why small stations lose their service in timetable revisions that are presented as improvements in journey time. The third constraint is the connection at the junction with the main line. Passengers judge a branch line almost entirely by whether its trains meet the expresses, and a branch train that arrives four minutes after the express has left is, for most travellers, an hour late. Planners treat the main-line times as fixed and work backwards, which means that the crossing pattern on the branch is ultimately dictated by a timetable written in another office for another purpose. When the main-line times move by a few minutes, as they do every December, the branch has to be replanned from the junction outwards. The fourth constraint is staff. A driver may work a limited number of hours without a break, must begin and end the shift at a depot where a relief is available, and cannot be asked to drive a type of train for which the route knowledge has lapsed. A timetable that is perfect on paper can be impossible to staff if it leaves a driver at the far terminal at the end of the permitted hours with no train back. Diagrams for trains and diagrams for crews are therefore drawn together, and a change that saves a trainset can cost a driver. The last constraint is recovery time. No timetable survives contact with weather, with doors held open, or with a freight train running out of course, so planners add minutes that are not needed in ideal conditions. Where those minutes are placed matters. Padding before a crossing loop protects the opposing train; padding before the junction protects the connection; padding at the terminal protects the next round trip. Padding spread evenly along the line protects nothing in particular and merely makes every journey slower. A sensible planner concentrates recovery time where a delay would otherwise spread, and accepts that trains will sometimes wait at a platform with the doors open and nothing to do. Now answer. First, explain why removing one stop can release a whole trainset, using the figures given. Second, say where you would place six minutes of recovery time on this line and why. Third, identify which of the five constraints is outside the control of the branch-line planner and describe the consequence when it changes.
